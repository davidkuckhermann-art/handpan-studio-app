Import from a screenshot

A student pauses a lesson, screenshots the notation table, and opens it in the app: the table is found, read, checked and placed into a piece they can play, slow down, loop and take to their own pan.

Why this can work well: the tables in the videos are the app's own notation drawn by one fixed template — one monospaced font, a known grid, two hand colours, a dark frame. A screenshot of one is close to ideal reading material. Every requirement on this page comes down to let the template be visible, and every limitation is a place where a video departs from it.

What the reader does

Nothing here needs a server. It runs in the page on a canvas, in well under a second, and it is deterministic: the same screenshot reads the same way twice.

  1. Find the frame. The table is a light rounded rectangle with a dark-brown border on a video that is mostly darker and softer. Its four corners are the whole calibration; everything after this works in table coordinates.
  2. Fix the grid. Rows from the height (the template's row is a constant share of the table width); slots from the pitch of the glyphs, which is one monospace advance, so a 12-slot triplet row and a 16-slot row are told apart without reading the header; counts from the vertical lines. The chips on screen 2 are this step's answer, and the student confirms it.
  3. Read each cell. Colour first: black, green or orange-red ink says the hand. Then the shape: the cell's ink is matched against the template's own glyphs (D, 1–9, M, ^, °, T, K, the dot, the tilde) rendered once from the notation font — a lookup, not a guess. Two or three small blobs stacked in one cell are a chord; a number on a grey disc is an underside note.
  4. Score every slot. A match that is not clearly better than its runner-up is marked uncertain, and that is what screen 3 shows in violet — the app never silently commits a doubtful cell.
  5. Build the events. Each cell becomes the app's own events at its t: the stack is same-t events, the hand is the colour token, K keeps its glyph — the transcription rulebook (TRANSCRIPTION.md) as code.
  6. Match the catalogue, if there is one. Optional but powerful: the string of cells is a fingerprint, and if it lands near a table David has already blessed, the app offers the verified piece instead of its own reading. See the engines below.

What a screenshot needs to be

Six frames a student might actually take. The verdicts are what the reader would say about them.

The requirements, as the app would state them to a student

  • Fullscreen and landscape, the video filling the phone. In the inline portrait player the table is a third of the width and its chord digits are five pixels tall.
  • Paused, on a frame where the table is fully on screen and not animating in or out.
  • Controls hidden — tap once after pausing. The scrubber crosses exactly where the table sits in many lessons.
  • The whole table in the picture with a little dark around it. A cropped table still reads, but only the slots it can see.
  • A screenshot, not a photo, when possible. A photo of a screen works with a perspective fix, but expect more slots to check.
  • One table per screenshot. Lessons that show two tables at once need two screenshots, or a crop.

Three engines, and which to build first

On-device reader first

Canvas and template matching, in the page. No server, no cost, no key, works inside the Kajabi embed and offline.

  • Deterministic and fast
  • Built for the v2 template only
  • Needs a set of real student screenshots to tune the thresholds — thirty would do
  • Roughly the size of one of the app's skins

The blessed catalogue with it

David's own transcriptions of his lessons, shipped with the app as a few kilobytes of fingerprints and pieces.

  • Every lesson he has blessed imports perfectly, whatever the screenshot quality
  • Uses the reader as the key, so it needs the reader first
  • Bootstrapped in the studio, where a vision model can do the first pass
  • New lessons should carry their piece as a code under the video — then no screenshot is needed at all

Vision model later

Claude reads the screenshot with the rulebook as its instructions and returns events.

  • Handles every generation, handwriting, chord names, odd frames
  • Needs a small server holding the key, behind the embed's gate
  • A few cents and several seconds per screenshot
  • Can misread confidently — the very rhythms the rulebook records — so the Check step stays

The Pick–Found–Check–Place flow is the same whichever engine reads the cells, so it is built once. The on-device reader goes in first because it needs nothing that does not exist yet; the catalogue turns it from "usually right" into "right on every lesson he has looked at"; the vision model waits for a backend and earns its place on the older lessons.
