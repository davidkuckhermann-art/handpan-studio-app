The settings drawer

David's idea: the bottom bar gets a handle, and swiping it up opens the settings that belong to whatever you are doing. One gesture, one place, in every mode — and several rows of buttons leave the main screen. This page proposes what goes in the drawer per mode, and two ways to solve the one hard case: editing, where the bottom already holds the writing tools.

The rules the drawer has to obey

The handle sits on the drawer's own upper edge, centred (David's call) — never at the screen's bottom edge, which belongs to iOS. A swipe that starts within a few millimetres of the bottom is the system's home gesture, and in a home-screen web app it wins. Starting the gesture on a handle that sits a whole bar-height above the edge keeps them apart.
Only the handle opens it. The tempo control is a vertical scrubber — drag up to speed up — and it lives in that bar. If a swipe anywhere on the bar opened the drawer, the two would fight. The menu sheet already works exactly this way, so the pattern is proven here.
Tap as well as swipe. The tap is what makes it discoverable; the swipe is what makes it fast.
What never goes in the drawer: the play button, the tempo, and the arrangement chips. Those are touched while the music runs — burying them would trade the win straight back.

The hard case: editing

Editing already uses the bottom of the screen as a tool surface — the bar lane, the input pan, the hand and stroke buttons. That surface has to stay open, because you tap it on every note. So the question is what a drawer even means here. Two answers, both drawn below.

Swiping up adds the settings above the tools and keeps the lane at the bottom, so you still see the bar you are writing into. The pan steps aside while you are setting things rather than placing notes — and one swipe down brings it back.
Why I would take Option B. One handle in one place in every mode is the whole point of the idea — a second handle makes the editor the exception you have to remember. B also keeps the gesture away from the screen's bottom edge, where iOS is listening, and it explains itself in one sentence: the drawer rests at whatever this mode needs on screen all the time, and swiping up shows the rest. Reading rests at the transport; editing rests at the deck; the gesture never changes.
What Option B costs. While the drawer is open in editing, the notation is behind it. That is fine for setting a time signature or the arrangement, and wrong for anything you do while looking at the music — which is why the hand, tone and stroke buttons appear in the open drawer as well as staying in the deck's chip row. The drawer is the roomy view of them, not their only home.

Before building

  1. Option A or B for editing? My recommendation is B, one handle at the same height in every mode, with the deck as its resting state.
  2. Does the drawer replace the ⋮ sheet, or live beside it? Beside it, with a clean line — ⋮ is you and your material (pan, transposed or not, library, share, modes), the drawer is what you are doing now. If that line ever feels arbitrary, the fallback is one sheet reached two ways.
  3. Does the groove selector belong in the drawer or stay on screen? It names what is playing, which argues for staying; it is also a button you press rarely once you have chosen.
  4. Landscape stays as it is — full-screen notation, its own bar. The drawer is portrait only unless you want it there too.
